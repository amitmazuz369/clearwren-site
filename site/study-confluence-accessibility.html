The accessibility of documentation published in Confluence

A great deal is known about the accessibility of home pages. Almost nothing is known about the documentation organisations actually publish. This is a first measurement.

Published 7 September 2026, widened 9 September, recounted 7 October. 1,082 pages across 12 public Confluence Cloud sites, measured against WCAG 2.2 by the Clearwren rule engine.

Corrected on 7 October

The first version of this page counted two habits as failures: a bare URL used as link text, and an H1 used inside the page body. A reader showed us that neither fails WCAG on its own. A URL names its own destination, and success criterion 2.4.4 lets the sentence around a link explain it. A second H1 is untidy, not a barrier. We also reported a table used only to lay out images as a data table without a header row, which it is not.

We fixed the checker and measured again on a fresh random sample. Those habits are now reported as advice for review, and they no longer count against a page. The earlier figure, "roughly half" of pages with no level A or AA failure, is replaced below.

A handful of checks account for most of what fails

This is the finding that matters, because it says the problem is smaller than it looks.

Every one of these is mechanical. None needs a specialist to find, and none needs a redesign to fix. A documentation estate failing on all of them is not badly built; it is unmeasured. Some are reported for a person to confirm rather than as certain failures: "click here" can be acceptable when the sentence around it says where it goes.

Two habits are more common than any of these, and neither fails on its own: bare URLs used as link text (921 instances on 286 pages, every site) and an H1 inside the page body (645 on 158 pages). Both are worth tidying. Neither is a barrier, and we no longer count them as one.

Each check, with the editor step that closes it, is written up in the checks behind most of it.

Contrast is concentrated, not spread

1,791 contrast issues sit on just 125 pages. That is almost always one template or one colour, chosen once and repeated. It is the largest problem by volume and one of the smallest to actually fix: change the value, not 125 pages.

The alt text problem is not the one people think

Missing alt text is the most widespread certain failure, on 249 of the 1,082 pages. That is the expected result.

The unexpected one sits below it: 231 further issues where alt text was written and does not describe the image. Filenames, the word "image", a caption repeated from the paragraph above. Somebody filled the field in and the information is still gone.

That distinction matters for how you fix it. The first group needs a policy. The second needs the authors to understand what the field is for, which is a different and slower piece of work.

Software docs are cleaner than knowledge bases

Half the sites are public knowledge bases, mostly in education; the other half are open-source software projects and research-computing docs, added to widen the frame. The software docs came in cleaner: 65% of pages with no A or AA failure, against 57% for the knowledge bases.

The difference is in the kind of failure. Missing alt text sat on 16% of the software pages against 32% of the knowledge-base pages. Bold used as a heading and skipped heading levels went the other way, on 15% and 10% of software pages against 7% and 4%. Those are the failures of writing fast in plain text, rather than the failures of a rich page.

No site was clean, and the range was wide

Per-site, the share of pages with no level A or AA failure ran from about a third to about four in five. The best site in the sample still had roughly one page in six carrying a failure; the weakest had more than two in three.

One failure appeared on every site: skipped heading levels. It comes from writing for how a page looks rather than how it is structured.

Method

  1. Site selection. Publicly readable Confluence Cloud sites, found through ordinary web search. All serve their content to anonymous visitors by their own configuration. The September runs covered thirteen; one did not respond during the October recount, which therefore covers twelve.
  2. Page selection. The full public page list was enumerated for each site, then a random sample of up to 100 pages drawn from it. This matters more than it sounds: an early run took the first pages of each space and reported 90% clean. Those are landing pages — short, no images, no tables. Sampling from the top of a documentation tree measures the part with the least content in it.
  3. Sampling error. At 100 pages per site the figure carries real noise. Re-drawing the same sites has moved it by up to eight points from the sample alone. Treat the precision as about plus or minus eight points, and the honest claim as "between a half and two thirds".
  4. Measurement. Page bodies were read in Atlassian Document Format, the same input the product uses, and evaluated by the same 32 rules across 15 WCAG 2.2 success criteria. No conversion step, so no fidelity lost. A failure is counted only where a rule is certain; everything that needs human judgement is reported for review and does not count against a page.
  5. Politeness. Each site's own robots.txt was read and its stated crawl-delay honoured. Only content served publicly was requested.
  6. Reporting. Aggregate only. No site is named, here or anywhere else.

What this does not show

Twelve sites is still a small sample, and they were not randomly selected from all Confluence sites — no such list exists. They are the sites that serve their content without an account, which is its own kind of selection.

Nor does it measure everything. Roughly a third of WCAG cannot be decided by any tool: whether reading order matches meaning, whether a description is adequate, whether captions are accurate. Those criteria are reported as needing review, never as passing.

So: treat this as a first measurement of a thing nobody had measured, not as a settled figure. We would rather publish a number with its limits attached, and correct it in public when we get something wrong, than a rounder one without them.

Why we ran it

We build an accessibility checker for Confluence, so we had both the engine and the reason to care. That is a conflict worth stating plainly. It is also why the method is written out above in enough detail to be repeated by someone who wants to check us.
