The accessibility of documentation published in Confluence

A great deal is known about the accessibility of home pages. Almost nothing is known about the documentation organisations actually publish. This is a first measurement.

Published 7 September 2026, widened 9 September. 1,179 pages across 13 public Confluence Cloud sites, measured against WCAG 2.2 by the Clearwren rule engine. The first version of this page covered seven sites; the notes on sample size below explain what the extra six changed and what they did not.

What we found

MeasureResult
Pages measured1,179
Sites measured13
Pages with no level A or AA failureroughly half (57% across the full sample; 49% when the original seven sites were re-drawn)
Average page score73 / 100
Distinct issues detected6,486
Of those, rated critical3,123

Read the headline figure as one page in two, not as a precise percentage. Re-running the same seven sites with a fresh random sample moved it eight points on its own; the section on method sets out why.

Six checks account for most of it

This is the finding that matters, because it says the problem is smaller than it looks.

Every one of these is mechanical. None needs a specialist to find, and none needs a redesign to fix. A documentation estate failing on all six is not badly built; it is unmeasured.

Contrast is concentrated, not spread

1,350 contrast issues sit on just 122 pages. That is almost always one template or one colour, chosen once and repeated. It reads as the second-largest problem by volume and is one of the smallest to actually fix: change the value, not 122 pages.

The alt text problem is not the one people think

Missing alt text is the largest single category, on 311 of the 1,179 pages. That is the expected result.

The unexpected one sits below it: 294 further issues where alt text was written and does not describe the image. Filenames, the word "image", a caption repeated from the paragraph above. Somebody filled the field in and the information is still gone.

That distinction matters for how you fix it. The first group needs a policy. The second needs the authors to understand what the field is for, which is a different and slower piece of work.

Software docs are cleaner than knowledge bases

The original seven sites were public knowledge bases, mostly in education. The six added in September are open-source software projects and research-computing docs. They came in cleaner — 65% of pages with no A or AA failure, against 49% for the re-drawn knowledge bases.

The reason is visible in the breakdown. The software docs carry far fewer images, so far less missing alt text. They carry more bare URLs pasted as link text and more bold-as-heading — the failures of writing fast in plain text rather than the failures of a rich page. Different content, different failure, similar total.

No site was clean, and the range was wide

Per-site conformance ran from the low forties to the low seventies. The best-performing site in the sample still had roughly a quarter of its pages carrying a level A or AA failure, and the weakest had more than half.

Two rules appeared on all 13 sites: bare URLs used as link text, and skipped heading levels. Both come from the same habit — writing for how a page looks rather than how it is structured.

Method

  1. Site selection. Thirteen publicly readable Confluence Cloud sites, found through ordinary web search. All serve their content to anonymous visitors by their own configuration. Seven are public knowledge bases; six are software-project and research-computing documentation, added to widen the frame beyond education.
  2. Page selection. The full public page list was enumerated for each site, then a random sample of up to 100 pages drawn from it. This matters more than it sounds: an earlier run took the first pages of each space and reported 90% clean. Those are landing pages — short, no images, no tables. Sampling from the top of a documentation tree measures the part with the least content in it.
  3. Sampling error. At 100 pages per site the figure carries real noise. Re-drawing the original seven sites produced 49% clean where the first published run had 57% — an eight-point move from the sample alone. Treat the precision as about plus or minus eight points, and the honest claim as "between a half and two thirds".
  4. Measurement. Page bodies were read in Atlassian Document Format, the same input the product uses, and evaluated by the same 32 rules across 15 WCAG 2.2 success criteria. No conversion step, so no fidelity lost.
  5. Politeness. Each site's own robots.txt was read and its stated crawl-delay honoured. Only content served publicly was requested.
  6. Reporting. Aggregate only. No site is named, here or anywhere else.

What this does not show

Thirteen sites is still a small sample, and they were not randomly selected from all Confluence sites — no such list exists. They are the sites that serve their content without an account, which is its own kind of selection.

Nor does it measure everything. Roughly a third of WCAG cannot be decided by any tool: whether reading order matches meaning, whether a description is adequate, whether captions are accurate. Those criteria are reported as needing review, never as passing.

So: treat this as a first measurement of a thing nobody had measured, not as a settled figure. We would rather publish a number with its limits attached than a rounder one without them.

Why we ran it

We build an accessibility checker for Confluence, so we had both the engine and the reason to care. That is a conflict worth stating plainly. It is also why the method is written out above in enough detail to be repeated by someone who wants to check us.
